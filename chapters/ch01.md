# Chapter 1. The Question Behind SGP

**The question that starts everything:** Why does mathematics describe our experience so well — and why *these* mathematical structures rather than others?

## What the Institute set out to ask

The Institute's governing document states the mission as a single question [SGP-commitment]:

> "Why does the mathematics of physics successfully describe the stable, coherent, interactive world that appears within sentient-conscious experience?" (GOVERNANCE.md:13–15)

Three observations motivate it (GOVERNANCE.md:23–31): human experience is remarkably stable; physics models large portions of that stability; physics is written in mathematics. The unanswered question is why mathematics is *capable* of doing this — not merely predicting observations, but organizing stable experience at all.

The same document is explicit about what the mission does *not* mean: not inventing new mathematics, not replacing physics, not proving any philosophical position, not hunting exotic objects (GOVERNANCE.md:33–38). Hold onto that list. It matters later, because parts of the research program drifted toward exactly these things, and the audits that corrected the drift are part of the story.

## Asking what exists vs. asking why reality has its character

Most science asks: what exists, and how does it behave? SGP asks something one level stranger: why does reality have the *character* it does — stable, coherent, mathematically describable — rather than some other character, or none?

This is the difference between asking "what are the laws?" and asking "why are there laws-shaped regularities for us to find?" The second question is ambitious in a way that makes working scientists nervous, because it is not clear what kind of answer could even count. A metaphysical interpretation? A philosophical argument? A mathematical constraint? A physical prediction? These are different levels of achievement (mission language the book keeps throughout):

- **Offering a metaphysical interpretation** — a coherent way of seeing things.
- **Providing a philosophical argument** — reasons to prefer that way of seeing.
- **Deriving a mathematical constraint** — a theorem that rules something out.
- **Explaining a physical phenomenon** — showing why an observation had to be so.
- **Producing a novel, falsifiable prediction** — saying something new that could be wrong.

The book never confuses these levels. Much of the Institute's history is the story of learning — sometimes the hard way — which level each result actually reached.

## A note on names and labels

Two warnings the records force on any honest account. First, the letters "SGP" collide: an early theory document defines a "Structured Geometry Program" with its own formula (`theory/SGP.md:1`), disjoint from the sentience-first program this book is about. Second, the commitment-labels collide: the "C1–C4" of the later mathematical grammar (relation iteration, hierarchy, composition, term rewriting) are *different objects* from the "C1–C5" philosophical commitments you will meet in Chapter 2. The book always says which one it means. (Full details: Appendices A–B; research-map 03-terminology-map, 07-contradictions.)

Relatedly, the archive mentions SFI, SFH-SGP, and SGI; whether these are distinct lineages or one project renamed is unresolved in the records (LOST_WISDOM_REPORT.md:249). This book says "SGP" for the sentience-first program and "the Institute" for the researchers, and leaves the genealogy question marked open.

## Where this chapter leaves you

The Institute began with a legitimate, deep question about mathematical intelligibility. Its answer-strategy was a bold reversal: start from sentience instead of matter. The next question is what that starting point actually means — because, as Chapter 2 shows, the records contain *two different answers*, and the difference changes everything downstream.
